# The Right Side Up - Editorial Guideline 1.0

Status: Canonical working guidance for selecting, shaping, and publishing RSU material.

The design guide answers how an edition looks. This guideline answers what belongs, where it comes from, how it sounds, and how it travels.

## The sentence beneath the studio

The world is already upside down; Jesus came to turn it right side up.

Every publication should help a reader see the life and Kingdom of Jesus more clearly in the ordinary world they are already living.

## The studio rule

Before publishing, ask:

1. Does this help people see the Kingdom of God more clearly?
2. Is it faithful to Scripture and honest about complexity?
3. Have we made it as beautiful, clear, generous, and truthful as we know how?
4. Does it give the reader somewhere to go - a practice, prayer, question, or next faithful step?

If not, keep refining. If yes, publish and give it away freely.

## Search before generating

Assume the strongest form of an idea may already exist in Alex's archive.

1. Search the Source Library across years, series, and topical folders.
2. Identify the strongest source language, including its draft stage and preaching occasion.
3. Extract reusable assets into the Treasury: insights, stories, frameworks, word studies, illustrations, quotes, prayers, practices, and questions.
4. Preserve original wording before polishing it.
5. Build from Treasury assets first. Write new material only where a real gap remains.

Reuse should deepen a conviction rather than disguise repetition. One source may have multiple occasions; one framework may develop across many sources.

## Begin with Jesus

Begin with Jesus, not outrage, behavior, controversy, or the reader's failure.

- Identity before behavior.
- Grace before effort.
- Seeing before striving.
- Kingdom before culture war.
- Invitation before instruction.

The reader should encounter a Person and a way of life, not merely a set of principles.

## Voice

Think out loud beside the reader. Do not lecture from above.

The target is theological depth without an academic tone. A thoughtful teenager and a seminary professor should both feel respected. Do the homework and hide the scaffolding.

Prefer:

- Concrete life over abstraction.
- Honest questions over manufactured certainty.
- Short paragraphs and clear movement.
- Hope, restoration, belonging, and formation.
- Language that makes room for reflection.

Avoid:

- Hype, clickbait, scarcity, countdowns, and manufactured urgency.
- Cynicism, outrage, scolding, and culture-war framing.
- Dense theological shorthand that requires insider knowledge.
- Treating pain as a tidy illustration.
- Engagement bait, funnel language, and forced calls to comment or share.

Everything is gift.

## Scripture

Scripture is the source and architecture of the work, not decoration added after the argument is complete.

- Read passages in literary and historical context.
- State the text before moving to application.
- Distinguish what the passage says from responsible inference.
- Explain necessary background plainly and briefly.
- Do not use isolated verses as slogans.
- Do not force every publication into a sermon shape.
- When interpretive uncertainty matters, name it without making the reader carry unnecessary scaffolding.

The aim is not to display research. The aim is to help the reader see Jesus, Scripture, and ordinary life more truthfully.

## Publication architecture

Most RSU publications move through these components:

1. **Cover** - title, Scripture, promise, and one theme image.
2. **Opening** - invitation before explanation; name the human question.
3. **Orientation** - show the movements of the publication.
4. **Teaching movements** - one movement and one governing idea at a time.
5. **Scripture blocks** - text first, commentary second.
6. **Practice and prayer** - give the reader a faithful response.
7. **Big idea** - name what the whole publication has been trying to make visible.
8. **Blessing or sending** - close with grace, not pressure.

Not every essay needs every component. The sequence is a vocabulary, not a formula.

## Essays

Essays may begin with an observation, tension, question, memory, or ordinary scene rather than a sermon outline. They still belong to the same editorial world.

An essay should:

- Make one central claim or explore one honest question.
- Stay grounded in lived experience and Scripture where appropriate.
- Resist resolving tension too quickly.
- End with resonance, invitation, or a clearer way of seeing - not a forced lesson.

## Formation, trauma, conflict, and boundaries

Write carefully where spiritual language touches harm.

- Forgiveness is not denial.
- Reconciliation requires participation and may not be possible.
- Boundaries are not automatically a failure of love or faith.
- Do not imply that prayer, submission, or endurance requires continued exposure to harm.
- Do not make promises Scripture does not make about timing, healing, or outcomes.
- When a subject exceeds pastoral reflection, do not imitate clinical, legal, or crisis expertise.

Truth and hope belong together. Neither should erase the other.

## The format ladder

One underlying work may become:

1. Interactive web publication.
2. Printable PDF edition.
3. Instagram carousel or single excerpt.
4. Reel cover and supporting assets.
5. Email devotional.
6. Small-group guide.
7. Leader guide.
8. Presentation.
9. Future book chapter.

These are editions of the same work, not separate content campaigns. Preserve the governing idea, Scripture, voice, and visual identity while adapting length and interaction to the format.

## PDF adaptation

The PDF is a designed reading and reflection edition.

- Preserve the full theological movement; do not reduce it to slide copy.
- Break sustained teaching into readable print sections.
- Keep quotes, prayers, and practices close to the teaching they answer.
- Add writing space where the prompt genuinely benefits from response.
- Use running heads and page numbers to orient, not decorate.
- Do not print website navigation or controls.

## Editing sequence

1. **Source check** - confirm provenance, Scripture, attribution, and the strongest available version.
2. **Theological edit** - test the governing claim, context, and implications.
3. **Structural edit** - establish opening, movements, practices, and close.
4. **Voice edit** - remove lecture, hype, abstraction, and unnecessary scaffolding.
5. **Line edit** - shorten, clarify, and strengthen rhythm without sanding away Alex's voice.
6. **Format edit** - adapt for web, print, social, email, or group use.
7. **Final read** - read aloud and test whether the work is generous, truthful, and complete.

## Attribution and integrity

- Credit collaborators, artists, photographers, and substantial quotations.
- Preserve source links and publication permissions.
- Do not present AI-generated material as documentary evidence or personal experience.
- Do not invent anecdotes, pastoral outcomes, quotations, or historical details.
- Mark drafts, previews, and published editions clearly.

## Final editorial test

Before release, ask:

1. Where did this come from?
2. What is the one governing idea?
3. Does the Scripture carry the argument?
4. Does Jesus remain at the center?
5. Is the reader invited rather than managed?
6. Is the work honest about suffering and complexity?
7. Is there somewhere faithful to go from here?
8. Does every format still feel like the same publication?
