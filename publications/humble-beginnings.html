An Invitation

If you were offered anything, what would you ask for?

Imagine being handed a blank check. No catch. No script. Just one question from God: “Ask for whatever you want me to give you.”

That is the position Solomon is in at the beginning of 1 Kings 3. He is young, unproven, and standing in the shadow of his father. His answer will reveal what is already taking shape within him.

Real wisdom doesn't start with what you know. It starts with the reverence to admit what you don't, and the trust to ask the one who does.

The Bible has a word for wisdom: hokmah—skillful living. It is the difference between knowing a fact and knowing how to move through your life. Proverbs gives us the sayings of the wise. The stories show us wisdom in practice—or the absence of it.

But wisdom is not only a set of skills to master. It is a person to know. Jesus does not teach the wisdom of God from a distance; he is the wisdom of God embodied. Solomon asked for wisdom. We get to know the one Solomon was asking for.

Before money, work, anger, love, or pride, everything rests on this foundation.

Reverence

Wisdom does not start with information. It starts with wonder.

Read More ↓
Wisdom Starts With Wonder Proverbs 1:7

“Fear of the Lord is the foundation of true knowledge, but fools despise wisdom and discipline.” (Proverbs 1:7)

The Hebrew word behind “fear” is יִרְאָה — yirah. It can describe terror, but it also carries respect, reverence, and awe.

Think about the ocean: beautiful, terrifying, powerful, and worthy of respect. You do not control it. You learn how small you are beside it without becoming insignificant.

Proverbs calls this the foundation. Everything else the book says about money, speech, work, anger, love, and pride stands on it.

Psalm 8 gives language to that wonder: “When I consider your heavens, the work of your fingers, the moon and the stars, which you have set in place, what is mankind that you are mindful of them, human beings that you care for them?” (Psalm 8:3–4).

Wisdom doesn't start with information. It starts with wonder.

And wonder says the thing our pride never wants to say first: I am not God.

Practice

Where do you become aware again that God is immense and you are not? Make room to stand there long enough for wonder to return.

Prayer

God, meet me in what I cannot take in. Let wonder be my first response, and remind me that I am not you.

Humility

What do you do when you're the one who's supposed to already know?

Read More ↓
The Courage To Not Know Proverbs 3:5 · 1 Kings 3:7

Reverence toward God is only half the foundation. The other half is what you think about yourself.

Ancient Near Eastern royal ideology ran on a familiar script: the king was the source of wisdom for his people, not a recipient of it. Kings boasted that the gods had given them wisdom from birth. Strength and power were projected; weakness was hidden.

Solomon breaks that script out loud, on the record, to God's face: “I am only a little child and do not know how to carry out my duties.” (1 Kings 3:7)

Solomon is not saying he is dumb or incapable. He is David's son, raised in the palace and surrounded by experienced leaders. He is saying something more significant: even with everything I have been given, I am still not enough for this.

That is humility. It is not thinking less of yourself. It is thinking rightly about yourself—knowing what you bring to the table and knowing where it runs out.

Proverbs 3:5 says, “Trust in the Lord with all your heart; do not depend on your own understanding.” It does not say you have no understanding. It says not to lean your full weight on it. Eventually you will reach the edge of your experience, instincts, and ability to see what is really happening.

Wisdom has the courage to say, “I don't know.”

Practice

Where have you reached the edge of your own experience? Practice saying the honest words: “I don't know.”

Prayer

Lord, I do not know what to do here, and I do not have to pretend that I do. Teach me to trust you.

03

A Listening Heart

What do you ask for once the myth of self-sufficiency is gone?

Read More ↓
Lev Shomea Proverbs 3:6 · 1 Kings 3:9–13

“Seek his will in all you do, and he will show you which path to take.” (Proverbs 3:6)

Solomon asks, “Give your servant a discerning heart to govern your people and to distinguish between right and wrong.” (1 Kings 3:9)

The Hebrew behind “discerning heart” is literally לֵב שֹׁמֵעַ — lev shomea, “a hearing heart.”

Not a mind that calculates faster. Not a memory that holds more. A heart built to keep hearing from someone else, on purpose, for the rest of its life.

That request pleases God because it is the one request that admits it will need to keep being answered—over and over again. Solomon does not ask to graduate from needing God. He asks for a life of continual dependence.

Wisdom is a lifetime of listening.

Because Solomon does not ask for long life, wealth, or victory over his enemies, God gives him wisdom—and the things he did not ask for as well. Solomon needs the kind of character that can carry the weight of kingship. Wisdom is what makes that weight bearable.

Practice

Before the decision, the text, or the next confident assumption, stop and ask: “God, give me a heart that listens.”

Prayer

Give me a heart that listens. Keep me dependent on your wisdom today, tomorrow, and every day after that.

Think · Feel · Do

Ask Before You Assume

Think: Where are you trying to figure it out on your own? Your marriage, your kids, a friendship, school, work, money, or a decision? Maybe you think you already know what needs to happen. Maybe you have no idea. Either way, the question is the same: am I leaning on what I know, or listening to God?

Feel: Saying “I don't know” can feel terrifying or weak, so we sometimes project strength and wisdom we do not actually have. But there is freedom in admitting, “God, I don't know what to do here, and I don't have to pretend that I do.”

Not: I know what I'm doing. But: I know who I'm listening to.

Do: Before you make the decision, send the text, or decide you already know what to do, stop and pray: “God, give me a heart that listens.” Then listen. Tomorrow, do it again. Ask before you assume. The wise never graduate from needing God.

That is the tragedy of Solomon's story. The young king asks for a heart that listens, and God gives him wisdom. But somewhere along the way, the man with the listening heart stops listening. The king who said “I don't know” begins living like he knows best. The wisest man in the world is not wise enough to save himself.

Neither are we. We need more than good advice, more than Proverbs, and more than trying harder. We need someone greater than Solomon.

Jesus said, “The queen of the South... came from the ends of the earth to listen to Solomon's wisdom, and now something greater than Solomon is here.” (Matthew 12:42)

Solomon stopped listening. Jesus never did. Solomon went his own way. Jesus trusted the Father all the way to the cross. We do not come to Jesus because we have figured it out. We come because we need him.

Wherever you are standing in front of something too big to manage, may wonder be your first response—not your last resort. And may God give you a heart that keeps listening.
