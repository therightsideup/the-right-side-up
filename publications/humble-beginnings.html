An Invitation

If you were offered anything, what would you ask for?

Not hypothetically. Imagine the offer was real — no budget, no catch, just: name it. Most of us have run that daydream. It's a fun game to play, right up until someone actually asks you to answer for real.

That's the exact position Solomon is in at the start of 1 Kings 3. Young. Unproven. Following a father whose reputation could crush anyone standing next to him. And in the middle of the night, God shows up and says it plainly: ask for whatever you want.

Whatever Solomon says next is going to tell everyone — and him — exactly who he is.

Real wisdom doesn't start with what you know. It starts with the reverence to admit what you don't, and the trust to ask the one who does.

The Bible has a word for this kind of wisdom: hokmah — skillful living, the difference between knowing a fact and knowing how to actually move through your life. Proverbs hands you the theory. The stories around it hand you the practice — real people, some getting it right and some not, sometimes the same person at different points in their own story.

And every week, this series will keep landing in the same place: wisdom isn't only a set of skills to master. It's a person to know. Jesus doesn't just teach the wisdom of God from a distance — he is the wisdom of God, embodied (1 Corinthians 1:24; Colossians 2:3). Solomon asked for wisdom. We get to know the one Solomon was only ever asking for.

This is where the whole Wisdom IRL series has to start, then — not with a skill, but with the posture underneath every skill that follows.

01

Reverence

What happens when you stand in front of something too big to manage?

Read More ↓
Wisdom Starts With Wonder Proverbs 1:7

Picture a call from someone who could give you anything. No budget. No catch. Just: name it. That's the position Solomon is in, at the very start of his reign.

In the middle of the night, God asks him plainly: “Ask for whatever you want me to give you” (1 Kings 3:5). Whatever Solomon says next will tell everyone — and him — exactly who he is.

The Hebrew word behind “fear” here is יִרְאָה — yirah. It isn't the word for being afraid of something that might hurt you. It's the word for what happens when you stand at the edge of something too vast to take in — a posture, not a terror.

Proverbs puts this word first on purpose. It calls it the foundation — everything the book later says about money, speech, work, and anger gets built on top of this one brick. Skip it, and the rest doesn't hold.

Notice where Solomon is standing when the offer comes — not on a throne yet, not with a temple built, not with a résumé. A young king at a worship site, sacrifices still in the air. Reverence was already in motion before the offer was ever made.

There's a place on the Oregon coast — Olivia Beach, in Lincoln City — where a long wooden walkway drops you down onto the sand. Every time I've stood there, I'm floored. Watch the people below from that walkway and they shrink to nothing against the size of the ocean, the wind, the roar of it. It's the kind of place that brings Psalm 8 to mind before you've even finished forming the thought: “When I consider your heavens, the work of your fingers, the moon and the stars, which you have set in place, what is mankind that you are mindful of them, human beings that you care for them?” (Psalm 8:3–4). Something happens when you actually let the size of it register. It levels you.

Wisdom doesn't start with information. It starts with wonder.

And wonder says the thing our pride never wants to say first: I am not God.

Practice

Where's your Olivia Beach — the place the size of things finally gets through to you? What does standing there actually do to you?

Prayer

God who cannot be managed — meet me in what I can't take in, and let wonder be the first thing I feel, not the last.

02

Humility

What do you do when you're the one who's supposed to already know?

Read More ↓
The Courage To Not Know Proverbs 3:5 · 1 Kings 3:7

Reverence toward God is only half the foundation. The other half is what you believe about yourself.

Ancient Near Eastern kings didn't talk like this. King Sulgi of Ur once claimed he'd been endowed with wisdom by the gods in his mother's womb — the wisdom to command men and fight like a warrior. A king was supposed to be the source of wisdom for his people, not a recipient of it. The posture was strength. Never weakness.

Solomon breaks that script out loud, on the record, to God's face: “I am only a little child and do not know how to carry out my duties” (1 Kings 3:7). For a king, in that culture, this is close to unthinkable. The one moment he's supposed to project competence, he confesses the opposite.

Solomon wasn't an idiot — David's son, raised in the palace, surrounded by some of the best minds in the kingdom. He isn't saying he's incapable. He's saying something far more significant: even with everything I've been given, I'm not enough for this. That's humility. Not thinking less of yourself — thinking rightly about yourself. Knowing what you bring to the table, and knowing exactly where what you bring to the table runs out.

Proverbs 3:5 asks exactly this of us: “Trust in the Lord with all your heart; do not depend on your own understanding.” It doesn't say you don't have understanding. It says don't lean your weight on it — it won't stand. Most of the damage in our lives doesn't come from a lack of information. It comes from trusting our own read on a situation past the point that read can be trusted.

Wisdom has the courage to say, “I don't know.”

Practice

Name a decision where you were completely confident in your own read on things — and your confidence turned out to be the problem, not the missing piece.

Prayer

Lord, I'm in over my skis more often than I let on. Take the competence I'm protecting and give me something truer to stand on.

03

A Listening Heart

What do you ask for once the myth of self-sufficiency is gone?

Read More ↓
Lev Shomea Proverbs 3:6 · 1 Kings 3:9–13

So give your servant a discerning heart to govern your people and to distinguish between right and wrong (1 Kings 3:9). The Hebrew behind “discerning heart” is literally לֵב שֹׁמֵעַ — lev shomea, “a hearing heart.”

Not a mind that calculates faster. Not a memory that holds more. A heart built to keep hearing from someone else, on purpose, for the rest of its life.

That's the request that pleased God — not because it was clever, but because it was the one request that admitted it would need to be answered again. Solomon didn't ask to graduate from needing God. He asked for an organ built to keep listening.

“Seek his will in all you do, and he will show you which path to take” (Proverbs 3:6). And God's response is worth sitting in: because Solomon didn't ask for a long life, wealth, or the defeat of his enemies, he's given wisdom — and all of that besides, unasked.

Humility doesn't just avoid a trap. It opens a hand wide enough to receive more than the hand that was grabbing ever could.

The request that pleases God is the one that admits you'll need to keep asking.

Wisdom was never a finish line Solomon could cross and be done. It was a life of continual dependence, asked for on purpose. Wisdom is a lifetime of listening.

Practice

Where in your life right now would it cost you the most to say, out loud, “I don't actually know what I'm doing”? That's usually where a listening habit needs to start.

Prayer

Give me a heart that listens, not a mind that's finished learning. I'd rather keep asking than stop needing you.

Issue One

Ask Before You Assume

Solomon asked for wisdom, and God gave him more than he asked. Not because the request was clever — because it was honest. He named exactly what he didn't have, and God met him there.

That's the pattern this whole series will keep returning to. Not confidence. Not competence. Wonder first — then a hand open enough to receive what you can't earn.

So where are you actually trying to figure it out on your own right now? A marriage. A kid. A friendship. Money. A decision you've already half-made. You might already think you know what needs to happen. You might have no idea at all. Either way, it's the same question: are you leaning on what you know, or listening to God?

Admitting you don't know can feel like weakness, so most of us perform a confidence we don't actually have. But there's real freedom on the other side of that performance — God, I don't know what to do here, and I don't have to pretend that I do. Humility was never the absence of confidence. It's confidence aimed at the right place.

Not: I know what I'm doing. But: I know who I'm listening to.

This week, before the decision, before the text, before you say the thing you'll regret — ask before you assume. Ask like it matters, because it does. The wise never graduate from needing God.

That's the tragedy hiding inside Solomon's own story. The young king who asked for a heart that listens becomes famous for wisdom — and somewhere along the way, the man with the listening heart stops listening. The king who said “I don't know” starts living like he knows best, and the story falls apart. The wisest man who ever lived wasn't wise enough to save himself.

Neither are we. We need more than good advice, more than Proverbs, more than trying harder — we need someone greater than Solomon. “Something greater than Solomon is here,” Jesus said of himself (Matthew 12:42). Solomon stopped listening. Jesus never did. Solomon went his own way, all the way to ruin. Jesus trusted the Father, all the way to a cross.

Wherever you're standing in front of something too big to manage this week — may wonder be your first response, not your last resort. And may you keep asking, because the one you're asking never stops listening either.
