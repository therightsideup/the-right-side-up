The Work

Forgiveness makes healing possible.

An invitation

Something has been torn.

Every wound creates two stories: what happened between us and what began happening within us because of it.

We may carry the memory of a sentence, a betrayal, an absence, or a promise that was not kept. Long after the moment has passed, the wound can keep shaping our expectations. We protect ourselves before danger arrives. We rehearse what should have happened. We imagine that healing will begin when the other person finally understands.

Sometimes they do. Sometimes they do not. So where does the work begin when we cannot control another person’s honesty, remorse, or willingness to change?

Forgiveness makes healing possible. Repair requires participation.

Paul writes to a community learning to live as people who have been made new in Jesus. His language is practical and deeply relational: put away what destroys life together, put on what reflects Christ, and forgive as you have been forgiven.

This is not an invitation to deny what happened. It is an invitation to let Jesus meet us where the damage has taken root.

01 · What Starts Within · Colossians 3:5–9

Tell the
Truth

What has this wound begun forming in me?

Look within

What starts inside eventually shows up between us.

Paul names desires that turn inward and make the self the center: greed, distorted longing, and the quiet idolatry of needing life to bend around us. Then he names what those desires produce in relationship: anger, rage, malice, slander, corrosive speech, and lies.

The lists belong together. The damage we do with our words and reactions rarely appears from nowhere. Something has been fed beneath the surface—a fear, an entitlement, a hunger to be vindicated, or a refusal to release control.

A wound can explain our reaction without becoming permission for it.

Self-examination is not self-condemnation. It is how we stop handing our unexamined pain to the people around us. We become curious about what the conflict is revealing: Why does this have such power over me? What am I protecting? What have I begun to believe about myself, the other person, or God?

The first movement toward healing is not pretending we are unharmed. It is telling the truth about both the harm done to us and the life it has begun producing within us.

Practice

Pray the words of Psalm 139: “Search me, God, and know my heart.” Name the wound honestly. Then ask what response, habit, or story has grown around it.

Prayer Jesus, search me without shaming me. Show me what pain has planted, and free me from passing it on.

02 · Put Off and Put On · Colossians 3:9–11

Wear the
New Life

Who am I becoming beneath the weight of what happened?

A new identity

Christian change begins with what Jesus has already made true.

Paul describes transformation like changing clothes. The old self is put off. The new self is put on. This is resurrection language: the life we once wore no longer names us, and the life of Jesus becomes the pattern into which we are being remade.

We often begin with behavior: try harder, react better, forgive faster. Paul begins deeper. You belong to Christ. Your wound is real, but it is not your identity. Another person’s failure may have marked your story, but it does not have authority to name the person you will become.

We do not forgive by willpower alone. We forgive from a new identity.

In this new humanity, the distinctions we use to rank, exclude, and protect ourselves lose their power. Christ is all, and Christ is in all. The person before us cannot be reduced to an enemy, a category, or the worst thing they have done. Neither can we.

Putting off the old self may mean releasing the role we learned to survive: the one who never needs anyone, the one who keeps score, the one who punishes with distance, or the one who disappears to keep the peace.

Practice

Name one old garment you have been wearing in this relationship. Then name one truth about who you are in Christ that you want to practice instead.

Prayer Jesus, loosen what no longer belongs to me. Teach me to live from the new life you have already given.

03 · The Work of Repair · Colossians 3:12–14

Make Healing
Possible

What belongs to forgiveness, and what requires both of us?

Chosen, holy, loved

Paul names the clothing of a people secure in the love of God.

Compassion. Kindness. Humility. Gentleness. Patience. These are not techniques for appearing agreeable. They are the habits of people who no longer need every interaction to prove their worth.

Then Paul speaks directly: bear with one another and forgive as the Lord forgave you. Forgiveness is the decision to release our claim to revenge and entrust judgment to God. It refuses to let another person’s sin keep governing our inner life.

Forgiveness can begin in one heart. Repair needs two willing people.

Repair asks for more: truth, responsibility, changed behavior, patience, and the slow rebuilding of trust. Forgiveness can open the door to that work, but it cannot complete the work by itself.

If the other person will not tell the truth, honor a boundary, or stop the harm, reconciliation may not be possible. You can release revenge without restoring access. You can forgive without pretending trust exists. You can love someone and still choose a distance that protects what is vulnerable.

Boundaries are not a failure of forgiveness. They can be one of the ways truth and love refuse to cooperate with continued harm.

Practice

Ask which work is yours today: release revenge, tell the truth, offer an apology, establish a boundary, or accept an invitation to rebuild. Do not demand from yourself what requires another person’s participation.

Prayer Jesus, make healing possible in me. Give me courage for the work that is mine, wisdom for the boundaries I need, and freedom to leave the outcome with you.

The big idea

Forgiveness makes healing possible.

Repair requires participation.

Our life together

Love is what holds the new life together.

Paul places love over every other virtue because love keeps compassion from becoming sentiment, truth from becoming cruelty, and boundaries from becoming revenge.

Love does not call evil good. It tells the truth about what happened while refusing to let the wound become the final author of our lives.

Healing is not forgetting the tear. It is refusing to let the tear decide what the whole cloth will become.

Sometimes love moves toward a difficult conversation. Sometimes it waits for evidence of change. Sometimes it blesses from a necessary distance. In every form, the aim is the same: to become people whose life together increasingly resembles Jesus.

Release

What claim to revenge am I ready to place in God’s hands?

Repair

What honest action belongs to my side of the relationship?

Boundary

What limit would protect truth, love, and what is vulnerable?

Prayer Jesus, clothe us with compassion, kindness, humility, gentleness, patience, and love. Make our life together a witness to your healing work.

Colossians 3:14

Over all these virtues put on love.

Love binds them all together in perfect unity.

A blessing for the work of repair

May we tell the truth without surrendering to shame.

May we release what revenge cannot heal.

May we become willing participants in the work of repair.

May the love of Jesus hold together what grace is making new.
